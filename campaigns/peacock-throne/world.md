# The Peacock Throne: world bible

This is where the depth lives. Descriptions in the chapters, people and places are selected
from here, never invented on the spot. Player-safe except the final section.

## The city, as a place to stand in

Sikri is built on a sandstone ridge above a plain. Everything official is red: the walls, the
gates, the Diwan-i-Am where the Padishah sits on Thursdays, the five-storey Panch Mahal whose
176 pillars hold up nothing but shade and wind. The one white thing is the marble tomb of the
saint by the great mosque, where people tie red threads through the screens to ask for
children, rain, or the return of someone lost. The threads are the first thing a child notices.

North-west of the ridge there is a lake, and the lake is the city's whole problem. The city
drinks from it through channels and Persian wheels turned by oxen, and every summer it is a
little lower. Water-carriers (bhishtis) walk the streets with leather bags on their hips and
sell by the cupful. They go into every house in Sikri, which is why they know everything.

The hours are kept by the drum house over the Elephant Gate. Dawn: the Padishah shows himself
at the balcony window so the city knows he is alive, and the drums play. Noon: the bazaar
awnings come down against the heat and the monkeys own the roofs. The hour before sunset: kites.
Night: the water channels are the loudest thing in the city.

Smells by district: wet sandstone and rose attar in the palace; turmeric, hot ghee and dung-fire
in the bazaar; indigo and ash in the dyers' quarter below the south wall; pigeons and cold stone
in the baoli.

## What the city eats and does

Khichdi with ghee is what everyone eats, the Padishah included. Kebabs on festival days.
Sherbet cooled with ice that comes down from the hills packed in straw, a luxury so absurd that
children are allowed one cup a year, at Nauroz. Paan after every meal; red-stained teeth on
every grown-up. Jalebi from Bibi Chandni's pan at the bazaar mouth, fried in a coil and dropped
into syrup while you watch.

The pachisi court beside the Anup Talao pool is a giant board cut into the stone, and the
Padishah plays it with servants as the pieces. Any child who sees it understands immediately.
Kites are flown from every roof in the last hour of light; strings are coated with glass dust
for cutting, which is why the kite-maker's fingers are scarred.

## Nauroz

The new year falls at the spring equinox and lasts nineteen days. The palace is hung with
cloth of gold. On the first morning the Padishah is weighed on a great balance against sacks
of grain, then silver, then copper, and the weight is given away. Nobles bring gifts; the
Padishah must give back more. There is a market inside the palace for nine days where the
women of the court sell trinkets and the Padishah pretends to haggle. Elephants are painted
for the parade on the first evening: flowers on the forehead, silver caps on the tusks, bells on
the ankles. New clothes for everyone who can afford them, and for children who cannot, a new
length of thread for their kite.

## Animals of the court

The cheetahs have ranks, like soldiers. Each has a keeper, a hood, a cart to ride to the hunt,
and a daily meat ration by rank. The chief cheetah was promoted with drums. They are
blindfolded until the hunt and spend the rest of their lives asleep in the shade, chained by a
soft rope. A child can walk right up to one. The keepers will not stop her; the cheetah will not
wake.

The elephants have ranks too, and a register. The best are given names and painted. An elephant
can smell water underground and will dig for it with a forefoot; old mahouts follow elephants
to find wells. The Hiran Minar, the tower studded with stone tusks outside the walls, was built
for a dead elephant who was loved.

Parrots are kept in the gardens because they repeat what they hear. A court parrot that has
lived long enough is a record of everything said in its hearing. Nobody speaks near Mirza.

Langurs live on the roofs and are not pets. The vanara guild is the city's joke and its postal
service: a langur with a sash will carry a letter across the roofs faster than a horse goes
through the gates, for a mango.

## The colour, and what it is

Here is the thing nobody at court says aloud. When Sikri was founded, the djinn of the baoli
made a bargain with the first Padishah: the throne's thousand gems, which hold the city's
colour, in exchange for the promise that the well would never be let run dry. Colour, in this
world, is the water-luck of a place. A grey thing is a thing the water has left. The lake has
been falling for years. The gems have been going grey for months. These are the same fact.

## The people (depth)

**Shahzadi Zeb, 14.** Wants, this week: to find her brother's elephant before the sunset parade,
because she is the one who unlatched the stable gate when Badal asked her to, and if the court
learns a princess hears animals she will be married off to somewhere quiet. Fear: being
"settled". Hands: always inked; she is copying the elephant register because she believes the
old one is wrong about Badal's age. Contradiction: bossy and frightened in the same breath.
Opinion: thinks the astronomer is a fraud (he is not). History: her mother died when Azim was
born; she has been his mother since she was three.

**Padishah Azim, 11.** Wants: to play pachisi with people his own age, and for Badal to be
happy. Fear: that everyone is only pretending to like him. Hands: sits sideways on the throne,
one foot swinging. Contradiction: an emperor who asks permission. As the gems grey, he goes
quiet; by chapter 4 he cannot laugh. Opinion: thinks the Vizier is the kindest man at court.

**Vizier Qamar-i-Aina.** Born Qamar, son of a dyer in the indigo quarter. His father's hands and
forearms were blue to the elbow for life, as all dyers' are, and the one time his father was
summoned to the palace with a bolt of cloth, nobody would take it from his hands. Qamar
decided at nine that colour was a wall: on one side the people who wore it, on the other the
people who were stained by it. He apprenticed to a mirror-maker, learned to silver glass, found
the astronomer who taught him optics, rose. His mirror takes colour because he built it to.
Wants: a throne with no colour, so that a dyer's son can sit on it. Fear: his brother. Hands:
turns the small mirror on its chain so it always faces away from himself. Contradiction: the
most courteous man in Sikri, and the only one who cannot look at his own face. He is not
wrong that the wall exists. He is wrong about what to do with it.

**Daulat.** Qamar's older brother, still a dyer, with one hand gone grey from a mordant burn.
Sells his brother's mirrors at a stall nobody visits because Qamar sends him money that way
without having to see him. Wants: his brother back. Keeps Qamar's childhood drawing of a blue
peacock.

**Bibi Chandni.** Sweet-seller at the bazaar mouth for thirty-one years. Her stall is where the
water-carriers fill up, which makes her the best-informed person in the empire. Wants:
customers to say please, and a reason to close the stall for one day (she never has). Hands:
drops jalebi coils into syrup without looking. Opinion: thinks Hanuvant's guild is overpaid.
Feeds the heroes before telling them anything, and tells them everything.

**Captain Hanuvant.** Grey langur, red sash, a brass telescope he cannot focus. Wants: the right
for the guild to sit on the top floor of the Panch Mahal at noon, where the breeze is. Has
petitioned for it eleven times. Fear: that nobody will ever believe a monkey. Hands: stands
like a general and scratches like a monkey. Opinion: considers Bibi Chandni "a civilian."

**Hakim Fariduddin.** Astronomer and physician, keeps the top floor of the Panch Mahal (the
breeze the guild wants), where his astrolabes are too big for the stairs and were built in
place. Taught Qamar optics when Qamar was fourteen and keeps Qamar's first mirror, a hand
mirror that shows things slightly too clearly, in a locked drawer. Wants: to understand the
greying as a natural law. Fear: that it is his fault. Hands: polishes lenses on his sleeve.
Answers questions with questions, which Zeb finds unbearable.

**Lady Shabnam.** Djinn of the baoli, keeper of the bargain, made of the cold air that comes
up from deep water. Wants the lake kept. Speaks in echoes because that is what a well does.
**Firoz**, her son, the age of a five-year-old in every way that matters, hiccups colour onto
things and cannot control it. Wants: to come along.

**Badal.** Old, white, slow, the grandson of the elephant the tower was built for. Hears water
underground and has been pulling toward the baoli for weeks. Wants: for the small frightened
voice under the city to stop crying. Trumpets once per chapter, when it matters.

**Mirza.** Parrot, green, red collar, two hundred years by the register. Heard Qamar, aged
twenty, say in the empty Diwan-i-Am: "I will own this throne by owning its colours." Has not
spoken in a year because the mirror took his voice with the first gem. Says one word per
session.

## What the heroes are to this city

Visitors at a festival. Nobody knows them, which is why Zeb can trust them.

## Secret

- The grey is the water leaving. Restoring the gems restores the lake; the Vizier does not know
  this and would be horrified.
- Zeb unlatched the gate. Badal asked.
- Daulat's drawing is what breaks the Vizier in chapter 5.
- Fariduddin's locked drawer holds the first mirror, which cracks the second.
